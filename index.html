Home-game advantage in football matches

Is it real? How does it vary around the globe? Do social or economic factors play a role?

The Champions League is witness to a quite a few historic collapses in the 2nd leg of crucial matches, when a big lead gained in the 1st leg was completely overturned. Barcelona losing to Roma (0-3, after a 4-1 lead), Liverpool (0-4, after a 3-0), and PSG losing to Barcelona (1-6, after a 4-0) come to mind. One thing in common? The winners were playing at home, with a very supportive fan turnout.

One of the most widely discussed phenomena in football is the so-called home advantage: the idea that teams tend to perform better when playing in their own stadium. Factors such as crowd support, travel fatigue, familiarity with the pitch, and even referee bias have often been proposed as possible explanations for this effect. But is the effect real?

Does 'home advantage' exist?

By visualizing the difference in home and away points won by each team per game, we can observe whether playing at home consistently provides a measurable competitive benefit. The datasets used feature information for 88 countries and 1975 clubs in the period 2010-2016.

From the hostile fortresses of South America to the pristine pitches of Western Europe, the data confirms what football fanatics have known for a long time, that home advantage has a noticeable effect on the score line. But, the tails are quite long on either side: while most teams stand to gain from a home-ground game, some have a record of being at a disadvantage.

Regional variations

To see whether the effect varies across geographic regions, below is a plot of point difference distribution divided by geographic regions. Each color represents a continent.

A globally observable phenomenon, but its intensity and reliability vary dramatically depending on the region. The presence of left-sided tails across the board proves that home advantage is not an absolute rule, and the wildly different shapes of these curves indicate that geography and infrastructure severely alter its impact.

Established European football environments display a highly predictable home advantage, with point differences tightly clustered between 0.2 and 0.4. This low variance is likely due to standardized league conditions, uniform pitch quality, and relatively short travel distances across the continent.

Leagues in Latin America and Africa exhibit extreme volatility, featuring "fat tails" in their data that indicate massive home advantages frequently exceeding a full point. This could mean that grueling away travel, severe environmental shifts, and fiercely hostile stadium atmospheres heavily paralyze visiting teams.

The median Baltic team's unique lack of home advantage might be due to the region's compact geography and uniform climate, which would eliminate travel fatigue for visiting squads.

Corruption & home-advantage

It is our hypothesis that a society with more corruption would allow more opportunities to illegally influence match officials like referees and linesmen. Home-ground staff like security personnel and ball boys could also try to aid the home team, for example, by wasting time when a ball goes out.

In our dataset, there is a moderate correlation between the perceived corruption in a society and the home advantage enjoyed by its football teams. Partly because, in countries where institutional integrity is low, match officials are historically more susceptible to the intense social pressure of a hostile home crowd. The weaker the national institutions, the more the referee appeases the immediate, aggressive local majority to avoid confrontation.

Economic influences

This section explores how different socioeconomic factors may be related to football results across the countries included in the dataset. More specifically, the aim is to analyze whether differences in living conditions, economic development, and employment structure are associated with variations in home advantage. These factors may influence the atmosphere around football matches, the level of local support, travel conditions, stadium environments, or other contextual aspects that can create more or less favorable conditions for home teams.

The strongest correlations with home advantage are found for GDP per capita and the percentage of the population working in agriculture or services. The remaining socioeconomic variables are less useful for the analysis, since most countries are highly clustered and only a few outliers stand out. As shown in the plot, GDP per capita and the share of the population employed in services are negatively correlated with home advantage. In contrast, the share of the population working in agriculture shows a positive correlation. These relationships are coherent, since countries with a higher dependence on agriculture tend to be less economically developed, whereas countries with a larger service sector usually have higher levels of development. Therefore, both variables seem to reflect similar underlying socioeconomic patterns.

It is also interesting to observe that the correlation between agricultural employment and home advantage is almost the opposite of the correlation between service employment and home advantage. This suggests that countries with a stronger industrial profile may occupy a more neutral position between the two extremes. This interpretation is also supported by the fact that the percentage of the population working in industry is the least correlated of these three employment-related variables with home advantage.

To sum up, these three factors suggest that countries with lower levels of socioeconomic development tend to show a stronger home advantage. In this context, football may have a stronger social and emotional role, acting as a form of collective identity, local pride, or escape from everyday difficulties. As a result, home support may become more intense, creating a more favorable atmosphere for local teams and potentially increasing the pressure on visiting teams.

Interactive Map

In this section, you can explore how club-level home advantage compares to country-level indicators, and whether there are any interesting patterns that emerge from this comparison.

In the map available below, you can click in your country and then in your favorite club to see how their home advantage compares to the average home advantage of the country across the period available in the dataset. Home advantage is measured with two metrics: the home/away point difference per game and the away goal difference per game. The first metric explicitly says how better (or worse) are your results at home, while the second one is a way to measure how different is your performance while playing outside.

If we analyze the case of some teams we can spot interesting patterns. For example, in Spain, we can see Atlético de Madrid is usually under the average home advantage of the country. This could be a consequence of the influence of Real Madrid, a much more popular club in the same city. However, we can see two strong peaks in point difference per game in 2010 and 2012. The peak in 2010 is easy to explain, because the club won the UEFA Super Cup, played at the start of the season, and made great and charismatic signings as Diego Godín, Filipe Luís, Diego Costa and Juanfran. In 2012, the team won the UEFA Super CUp another time, and Oliver Torres, from the youth team, made his debut in the first team.
